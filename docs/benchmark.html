Benchmarks

The PRESS benchmarks evaluate five modeling tasks, and the predictions composed from them, under a common match-level cross-validation protocol. This page describes the dataset, the two model families, the shared evaluation design and the performance metrics. Each task-specific benchmark reports its cohort, its model-specific training and calibration choices and its predictive results.

Research pipeline and source download · Project roadmap

1. Dataset overview

The benchmarks use ten A-League 2024/25 matches from SkillCorner Open Data (SkillCorner, n.d.). All component benchmarks concern outfield ball carriers. Reliability is checked at the temporal boundaries needed for the target. Two data-preparation branches share these principles: possession-based tasks use matched individual-control intervals, whereas pass tasks use matched release and the immediate next reception/recovery start. The matching with tracking data relies on the SkillCorner event labels, as described in Dataset and preprocessing.

For the possession branch used by ball-drive, action-selection and residual-risk training, the frozen preparation audit starts from 9,566 raw individual possessions. A possession is kept when its individual-control interval is matched with tracking data at both boundaries, its terminal event and outcome supply a supported component label, its carrier is an outfield player and it does not end in a shot. These criteria leave 8,451 possessions.

These are parent-possession counts before task segmentation (see Possessions and checkpoints). Individual tasks then select the actions and verified outcomes required by their target, and their benchmark pages give the task-specific criteria and the resulting cohort sizes. Features and predictions can still be computed for states outside these training populations. Effective exclusion counts and source-audit provenance.

Verified pass links

Pass-outcome verification links the matched release to the matched start of the immediate next individual possession, in the same match and period. The next control must follow release, with no intervening annotated interruption and complete observed ball XY over the interval. A completion requires the identified recipient to be the next carrier of the same team, with a matched reception; a failure requires an accepted opponent interception or recovery. The recovery location supplies the destination proxy for a failed pass. Only the matched release and the matched start of the next possession are required.

These checks establish the outcome and endpoint needed by the pass models. Flight-duration limits and the observations needed for trajectory fitting are task-specific and are stated in the corresponding benchmarks. Possession-based tasks additionally specify whether a failed verification excludes an entire parent possession or only an observation. Definitions of actions, checkpoints and contextual labels are centralized in Possession segmentation and context.

2. Model families

The tasks use two model architectures, which are developed and evaluated differently.

Physical models with estimated parameters. Ball-drive risk and pass probability have an explicit physical structure, and only a small number of parameters is learned from data by maximizing a likelihood. Model-selection choices, such as the strength of spline regularization, are few and are made on inner validation splits. The model produces probabilities directly, and any post-hoc calibration step is described in the task benchmark.

Gradient-boosted tree models. Pass selection, action selection and residual risk are XGBoost models trained on feature sets that include the outputs of upstream models. Their hyperparameters and number of trees are selected on a validation pair of matches by early stopping, and a probability calibration can be fitted afterwards.

The composed risk (h and xLoss) combines the predictions of the components.

Component notation

The model pages use the same component symbols when passing predictions between tasks. All quantities below are conditioned on the current state \(s\).

Symbol Meaning
\(q(s)\) Probability of choosing a pass; \(1-q(s)\) is the carry-choice probability
\(\pi_j(s)\) Probability of destination \(j\), given a pass attempt
\(C_{\mathrm{att}}\) Raw attacking-team pitch control, before pass-probability calibration
\(p_j(s)\) Probability of completing a pass to destination \(j\)
\(d(s)\) Loss probability during a canonical one-second carry
\(r(s)\) Terminal individual-possession loss probability conditional on a successful current carry
\(h(s)\) Current canonical-action loss probability, marginalized over choice and pass destination
\(\mathrm{xLoss}(s)\) Terminal individual-possession loss probability, including the residual continuation

3. Common evaluation protocol

Shared outer match folds

All five tasks use the same partition of the ten matches into five outer folds. Each fold holds out two complete matches and uses the remaining eight for development. The five test pairs are disjoint: every match, and every eligible observation for a task, contributes to the pooled test evaluation once. Task cohorts differ according to the target and the reliability checks required to observe it.

6 + 2 + 2 describes match roles. The two model families follow different development procedures within each outer fold:

Model family Development procedure within each outer fold Model evaluated on the two test matches
Physical models Four disjoint 6-fitting + 2-validation rotations within the eight development matches, used to select regularization or to produce cross-fitted scores for calibration Parameters refitted on all eight development matches
Tree models One fixed 6-fitting + 2-validation split, used for hyperparameter selection, early stopping and any calibration The six-match tree fit at the selected checkpoint

For the tree models, the two validation matches remain fixed throughout that fold’s search, early stopping and any calibration. The pair can change between outer folds because test matches must be excluded. The task benchmarks give the exact procedure of each task.

Hyperparameters, early stopping and probability calibration

For XGBoost, a hyperparameter configuration is trained on the six fitting matches, and its uncalibrated validation cross-entropy selects the tree prefix by early stopping. Where a grid is used, the best configuration is selected by this same native validation criterion. Numerical XGBoost predictors are used without standardization, and the categorical vocabulary and encoding are learned from fitting matches only.

Any temperature scaling is fitted after configuration/checkpoint selection. It minimizes validation choice or binary negative log-likelihood, with trees, selected hyperparameters and stopping point held fixed. The same two validation matches are reused for this calibration, so selection and calibration share the validation pair. Only after these choices are frozen is the corresponding outer-test score computed.

Features and dependent models

Some tasks use the outputs of upstream models as features. For a given outer fold, the upstream models are fitted on that fold’s fitting matches only, and the full-ten-match versions are used only for deployment. Snapshot quantities that require no learned parameters can be shared across folds, while model-dependent features have a separate version for each fold. Verified caches are reused with their fold-specific predictions, and full-data deployment weights enter only the deployment.

The validation pair supports both upstream calibration and downstream selection where those components are required. Fitting-row features can therefore be in-sample with respect to upstream models, so cross-fitting and independent validation apply layer by layer. Each task benchmark states which layers are refitted and which remain frozen.

Aggregation and performance metrics

Cross-validated performance is computed on the concatenation of the five outer-fold held-out prediction sets. Each observation contributes once to the pooled evaluation. Fold-level results serve to inspect stability, and the pooled estimates are the reported ones.

For the binary-outcome tasks, with observed labels \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) and predicted probabilities \(p_i\), the primary probabilistic metric is binary cross-entropy (CE), also called log loss:

\[ \mathrm{CE}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right]. \]

A task-specific null model supplies a reference cross-entropy \(\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{null}}\), estimated without the outer test labels and defined in each benchmark where needed. The relative improvement over the reference is

\[ \mathrm{Gain}=1-\frac{\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{model}}}{\mathrm{CE}_{\mathrm{null}}}, \]

and a positive gain indicates lower cross-entropy than the null model.

The Brier score is the mean squared probabilistic error:

\[ \mathrm{Brier}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2. \]

Calibration is summarized with the expected calibration error (ECE). Held-out predictions are divided into ten approximately equal-count quantile bins. For bin \(b\), let \(n_b\) be its number of observations, \(\bar p_b\) its mean prediction and \(\bar y_b\) its observed event frequency. Then

\[ \mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{10}\frac{n_b}{N}\left|\bar p_b-\bar y_b\right|. \]

Calibration plots compare observed event frequencies with mean predicted probabilities in these bins, and ECE and the bins are recomputed on the pooled outer-test predictions. Where relevant, ROC AUC assesses ranking across classes, and precision–recall AUC (PR AUC) summarizes discrimination for the positive class, particularly when outcomes are uncommon. Not every metric applies to or is reported for every task. Task-specific numerical conventions, including any probability clipping used for log loss, are recorded alongside the corresponding results.

Multiple-choice tasks are evaluated with the likelihood assigned to the observed choice among the available alternatives, and the improvement over a task-specific null choice model is summarized by McFadden’s pseudo-\(R^2\):

\[ R^2_{\mathrm{McFadden}}=1-\frac{\log L_{\mathrm{model}}}{\log L_{\mathrm{null}}}, \]

where \(L_{\mathrm{model}}\) and \(L_{\mathrm{null}}\) are the likelihoods of the observed choices under the fitted and null models, evaluated on the held-out observations. The task benchmark specifies its choice set and null model, and the nested cross-validation and the pooling of held-out predictions are unchanged.

Model stability and final refitting

The cross-validation results evaluate predictive performance and stability across the outer folds before a specification is accepted. The accepted specification is then refitted on all ten matches to obtain the parameters distributed with the PRESS package. The reported held-out estimates come exclusively from the cross-validation.

Deployment calibration may use labels paired with frozen cross-fitted predictions from all ten matches, after evaluation is complete. The saved benchmark predictions remain the reported held-out results. Final tree counts and configurations are chosen from validation records; the exact aggregation rule is task-specific.

Scope of the development benchmark

Outer-test labels are excluded from that fold’s model fitting, stopping rule and calibrator estimation. The ten matches were repeatedly examined during model development, so the complete development process serves as a development benchmark, and generalization to other competitions and annotation conventions remains open. The task benchmarks describe any development choice shared across folds and distinguish this scope from the match-disjoint fitting of the reported fold models.

The benchmark uses ten matches, with two held out in each outer fold. Observations within a match are dependent, so the number of independent units is the number of matches. Differences between models and between folds should be read with that uncertainty in mind. The same sample size motivates the choice of simple models with few parameters, as described in Modelling choices.
