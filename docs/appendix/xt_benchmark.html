Expected threat benchmark

Model and example · All benchmarks

The xT benchmark documents the training of the expected-threat surface and the selection of its grid resolution and smoothing bandwidths. The model vignette introduces the value equation, the fitted surface and its use in PRESS. This benchmark describes the event cohort, the training procedure and the selection, which relies on leave-one-match-out validation of the destination and goal-given-shot components.

1. Evaluation cohort

The production surface uses the archived event tables from all ten matches: 9,071 within-possession displacements, 6,355 completed passes, 212 shots (23 goals) and 2,214 terminal losses. A displacement records movement from the matched start to the matched end of individual control, and is distinct from the canonical one-second carries of the ball-drive classifier. Passes connect release to matched reception. Shot and loss locations use the end of individual control. Coordinates are oriented so the team in possession attacks from left to right.

These counts come from the archived event cohort, which is more permissive than the verified pass-probability cohort: it includes goalkeeper passers and passes later excluded there by the association and flight-duration checks, and it covers the completions whose origin and destination fall inside the fixed 105 × 68 m grid.

The components scored in the selection are the pass-destination, carry-destination and goal-given-shot estimates. The goal-given-shot component is the sparsest and rarest of them and is the one used to compare grid resolutions.

2. Training procedure

The fit proceeds in four steps. The event frequencies \(p_{a,i}\) are computed from the counts without smoothing. The destination distributions \(T\) and the goal probability \(p_{\mathrm{goal}}\) are estimated from smoothed counts. The smoothing bandwidths are selected by leave-one-match-out validation, as described in Smoothing bandwidth selection. Finally, the value equation is iterated to convergence.

What is smoothed

Gaussian smoothing is applied to the destination distributions and to the goal probability given a shot. In the formulas below, \(\varepsilon=10^{-8}\) is a small numerical constant.

  1. Destination distributions. For each fixed origin \(i\), the destination-count field \(N^a_{ij}\) is smoothed over neighbouring destination cells \(j\), separately for passes and carries. The smoothed counts are then normalized:

    \[ T^a_{ij}=\frac{\widetilde N^a_{ij}+\varepsilon} {\sum_k(\widetilde N^a_{ik}+\varepsilon)}, \qquad \widetilde N^a_{i\cdot}=K_{h_a}*N^a_{i\cdot}. \]

    Smoothing acts over destination cells, for each fixed origin. An origin with no observed transitions has a uniform destination row.

  2. Goal probability given a shot. The spatial goal counts \(G_i\) and shot counts \(S_i\) are smoothed separately, then divided:

    \[ p_{\mathrm{goal},i}=\frac{(K_{h_g}*G)_i}{(K_{h_g}*S)_i+\varepsilon}. \]

The four event frequencies \(p_{a,i}=n_{a,i}/n_i\) are computed directly from the counts. Bandwidths \(h\) are expressed in metres; the Gaussian standard deviations in grid units are \(h/\Delta x\) and \(h/\Delta y\).

Value iteration

The iteration of the value equation starts at zero and stops when the maximum change is below \(10^{-12}\).

3. Smoothing bandwidth selection

The smoothing bandwidth of each component (pass destinations, carry destinations and goal given shot) is selected by leave-one-match-out validation on the matches available for fitting. Whole matches are held out, so that each held-out set plays the role of a new match. The candidates are 0, 3, 5, 8, 12 and 18 m, where zero means no Gaussian smoothing. For a set of \(M\) matches the procedure is:

  1. Rotation. Each match is held out in turn. The component is estimated on the other \(M-1\) matches and scored on the held-out match. The score is the negative log-density of the observed destination for passes and carries, and the Bernoulli cross-entropy of the outcome for shots.
  2. Pooling. The \(M\) rotation scores of each candidate are combined with weights proportional to the number of events in the held-out match.
  3. Uncertainty. A delete-one-match jackknife of the paired score differences between each candidate and the best one gives the standard error of each difference.
  4. Choice. The largest bandwidth whose score lies within one standard error of the best is retained, so that the rule favours the stronger smoothing.

The same procedure is applied at three levels, which differ in the number \(M\) of matches that supply the fit.

Use Matches \(M\) Rotations Selected bandwidths
Choice of the grid, within each of the five outer folds 8 7 + 1 selected separately in each fold
Features for the benchmarks that use the 6 + 2 + 2 scheme, such as action selection and pass selection 6 5 + 1 8 m passes and 5 m carries in every fold; goal given shot 8 m in folds 0 and 4 and 5 m in the others
Production fit 10 9 + 1 8 m passes, 5 m carries, 5 m goal given shot

In the benchmarks that use the 6 + 2 + 2 scheme, the grid is fixed at 10 × 6 and the bandwidths are selected on the six fitting matches of each outer fold. xT is then refitted on the same six matches with the selected bandwidths, and this fit computes the xT features of the two validation and the two test matches. Held-out matches therefore never enter the selection, and each fold map uses the bandwidths selected in its own fold.

4. Grid resolution

Binning determines the spatial resolution of the fitted value function. Smaller bins preserve more local variation but provide fewer events per cell and sparser transition estimates; larger bins stabilize estimates but can obscure meaningful spatial structure. Smoothing can reduce sampling noise, at the cost of spatial detail.

The grid is chosen among four resolutions (8 × 5, 10 × 6, 12 × 8 and 16 × 12) on the event cohort of the ten matches. This is a second validation layer, built around the bandwidth selection above. The matches are divided into five outer folds, each with eight development and two test matches, and the test matches are disjoint across folds. For each resolution, every fold proceeds as follows:

  1. The bandwidths are selected on the eight development matches with the rule above (7 + 1 rotations).
  2. xT is refitted on the development matches with these bandwidths fixed.
  3. The goal-given-shot component, the sparsest and rarest one, is scored on the two test matches.

The out-of-fold scores are pooled with shot-weighted averages. A delete-one-match jackknife of the shot-weighted paired differences, with the out-of-fold predictions held fixed, gives the standard error of each resolution against 10 × 6. Among the resolutions within one standard error of the best, the coarsest is retained, which favours lower complexity. Positive ΔCE indicates worse performance than 10 × 6.

Grid CE Brier score ΔCE versus 10 × 6 Paired SE of ΔCE

The 10 × 6 grid has the lowest CE and is retained. The coarser 8 × 5 grid is clearly worse (ΔCE +0.0404, paired SE 0.0109). The 12 × 8 grid lies within one standard error of 10 × 6, which is the coarser of the two, and 16 × 12 has a ΔCE of +0.0096 (paired SE 0.0046). The finer grids have slightly lower Brier scores, while CE does not improve consistently. The comparison is based on the goal-given-shot component, and the complete long-term xT prediction is outside the scope of this validation.

5. Final fitted model

The production surface is fitted on all ten matches with a 10 × 6 grid and the bandwidths selected at the production level (8 m passes, 5 m carries, 5 m goal given shot). It is embedded in the deployed pass-selection and action-selection models, while the predictive benchmarks use xT fitted only on their corresponding training matches.

For reproducibility, the coverage, component scores, map stability and production provenance are provided as supplementary resources.
